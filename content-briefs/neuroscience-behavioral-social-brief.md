# Social Script & Hook Brief: Neuroscience and Behavioral Science

**Purpose:** A separate brief from the Cognitive Load and ADHD doc. Raw material for short-form scripts and videos, focused on how the brain and behavior actually work, and the newest findings about them. Same format throughout: a tension setup, audio lines built as a mini arc (hook, build, turn, land), the receipts to back it on camera, the idea voiced for every generation, and a practical fix to close on.

**Two halves:** Part A is the core behavioral mechanisms, the validated science of how your brain gets played. Part B is the newest 2023 to 2026 findings. Hooks lead with the concept name where it helps, because naming something people feel but cannot label is strong retention bait. No em dashes, every number sourced so creators hold up under a fact check.

## DOPAMINE: WANTING VS LIKING

**The tension:** Almost everything you have been told about dopamine is wrong.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "Dopamine is not the pleasure chemical. It is the wanting chemical. And that one correction explains why you cannot put the phone down."
- Build: "Craving and enjoying run on two different systems in your brain. Dopamine drives the chase, the seeking, the anticipation. A separate, smaller system handles the actual pleasure."
- Turn: "So apps do not need to make you happy. They just need to keep you wanting. You can scroll for an hour, enjoy none of it, and still reach for more."
- Land: "That is not a willpower problem. Your seeking system got hijacked by people who studied it."

**Your receipts:** Neuroscientist Kent Berridge's work separates "wanting" (incentive salience, dopamine-driven craving and seeking) from "liking" (actual pleasure, which runs on separate, dopamine-independent circuits) ([Berridge & Robinson, 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)). This is also why "dopamine detox" is a misnomer: you are not flushing a chemical, you are resetting habits. Never call dopamine the "pleasure chemical."

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "Dopamine is the wanting chemical, not the vibes chemical. Mind blown."
- Millennials: "You scroll for an hour, enjoy zero of it, and still want more. That is the wanting system."
- Gen X: "Craving and enjoying are two different switches. Apps flip the craving one."
- Boomers: "The itch to check is not the same as liking it. They built the itch."

**The fix (CTA):** "Add friction to the wanting, not guilt to the liking. Log out, take the app off your home screen, leave the phone in another room." You are not weak, you are up against your own seeking system, so make the chase harder. The craving fades when the cue is gone.

**Keep them watching:** Open with "everything you heard about dopamine is wrong," then make them wait for the wanting versus liking reveal.

## THE ILLUSORY TRUTH EFFECT

**The tension:** Repeat a lie enough and your own brain files it as true, even when you know better.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "There is a name for why a repeated lie starts to feel true. The illusory truth effect."
- Build: "In the studies, hearing a false statement a second time makes people rate it as more true. Even the people who can correctly tell you the right answer."
- Turn: "Your brain treats easy to recall as likely to be true. Familiarity feels like fact, no checking required."
- Land: "This is why slogans repeat and the same talking point shows up everywhere at once. Repetition is not emphasis. It is a technique."

**Your receipts:** Fazio et al. (2015) found repetition raised perceived truth even for statements people knew were false ([APA](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/xge-0000098.pdf)); the effect goes back to Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino (1977), has a medium effect size, and holds even for implausible claims. Caveat: it is a real nudge, not mind control, so do not say "repeat anything three times and people believe it."

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "Say it enough and your brain just files it under true."
- Millennials: "Familiar starts to feel factual. That is the whole misinformation game."
- Gen X: "Repeat a line often enough and it becomes common knowledge."
- Boomers: "Tell a lie often enough and people take it for the truth."

**The fix (CTA):** "When a claim feels obviously true but you cannot say where you learned it, treat that as a warning, not proof." Trace it to a source before you repeat it. Familiar is not the same as verified.

**Keep them watching:** Name the effect, then hit the unsettling part: it works even when you know the truth.

## ANCHORING AND PRE-SUASION

**The tension:** The first number you hear quietly drags every decision that follows.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "There is a name for why the first number you hear warps everything after it. Anchoring."
- Build: "Drop any number before a decision, even a random one, and people's estimates slide toward it. It works on prices, salaries, even how long you think a task will take."
- Turn: "The bigger idea is pre-suasion. What you pay attention to one second before a message changes whether you accept it."
- Land: "By the time the ask arrives, the ground was already tilted. You just did not watch it tilt."

**Your receipts:** Anchoring was one of the best-replicated effects in the Many Labs project (6,344 participants, 36 labs) ([Klein et al., 2014](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/1864-9335/a000178)); pre-suasion (Cialdini, 2016) is the idea that pre-message attention shapes acceptance. Caveat: cite anchoring, attention, and framing, which are robust, not subtle social priming, since the famous "elderly words slow your walk" study failed to replicate.

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "The first price you see is a trap for every price after it."
- Millennials: "Why does the menu have one absurdly expensive bottle? To make the rest look reasonable."
- Gen X: "Whoever sets the first number controls the negotiation."
- Boomers: "The asking price anchors you before you say a word."

**The fix (CTA):** "Before you decide, ask what number or image you saw right before, and set your own anchor first." Name the anchor and it loses its pull. Decide on the facts, not the framing you were handed.

**Keep them watching:** Hook on "the first number is a trap," then prove it with the menu or salary example.

## THE MERE EXPOSURE EFFECT

**The tension:** You start to like things just because you have seen them before, and you do not notice it happening.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "There is a name for why you like a song, a face, or a brand just because it is familiar. The mere exposure effect."
- Build: "Repeated exposure breeds liking, no reasoning involved. And here is the strange part. It works strongest when you never consciously noticed the thing."
- Turn: "That is why an ad does not need you to remember it. It just needs you to have seen it. Familiarity does the rest, under the radar."
- Land: "Comfort and quality are not the same thing, but your brain keeps confusing them."

**Your receipts:** Bornstein's meta-analysis of 208 experiments found repeated exposure reliably increases liking, with subliminally presented stimuli producing even larger effects than consciously seen ones ([Bornstein, 1989](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232497059_Exposure_and_Affect_Overview_and_Meta-Analysis_of_Research_1968-1987)); the effect traces to Zajonc (1968). Caveat: it is an inverted U, so overexposure can flip into annoyance; it is a nudge, not a switch.

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "You did not choose your favorite sound, the algorithm just played it 40 times."
- Millennials: "You like the brand because you have seen it everywhere, not because it is better."
- Gen X: "Familiar feels safe, so we keep buying familiar."
- Boomers: "You warm up to a thing just from seeing it around."

**The fix (CTA):** "Before you call something good, ask whether it is actually good or just familiar." Try the new option on its merits. Comfort is not the same as quality.

**Keep them watching:** Hook with "you like things for a reason you cannot see," then reveal the subliminal twist.

## EMOTIONAL CONTAGION

**The tension:** Moods spread like a cold, and a platform once proved it on you.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "There is a name for why one person's bad mood infects a whole room. Emotional contagion. And a platform once tested it on 689,003 people."
- Build: "Emotions transfer between people, often in seconds and without a word. You catch a vibe the way you catch a yawn."
- Turn: "In 2012 Facebook quietly filtered more positive or more negative posts out of feeds to see if it could shift people's emotions. It could. Nobody was told."
- Land: "Your feed is not neutral. It is an emotional climate, and someone else sets the thermostat."

**Your receipts:** Emotional contagion between people is well documented; Facebook's 2014 PNAS study altered the feeds of 689,003 users and shifted the emotional tone of what they then posted ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320040111)). Caveat: the experiment's effect was tiny (a few words per thousand) and measured posting, not diagnosed mood, and it was flagged, not retracted, so do not say it made people depressed. The story is the consent.

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "Your FYP sets your mood and you did not pick the thermostat."
- Millennials: "Ten minutes of angry Twitter and suddenly you are angry too. That is contagion."
- Gen X: "One sour coworker can turn the whole office. Feeds do it at scale."
- Boomers: "A bad mood spreads. The screen just spreads it faster."

**The fix (CTA):** "Treat your feed like an emotional diet and unfollow the accounts that reliably wreck your mood." You catch what you consume, so curate the climate. When you feel the vibe shift, put it down.

**Keep them watching:** Open on "they tested it on 689,003 people," then land the consent kicker.

## THE BANDWIDTH TAX

**The tension:** Stress does not just feel bad. It measurably lowers your thinking in the moment.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "There is a name for why money stress makes everything harder to think through. The bandwidth tax."
- Build: "When part of your mind is occupied by scarcity, whether money, time, or a crisis, there is less left for everything else."
- Turn: "Researchers measured it. Triggering financial worry dropped cognitive performance by about 13 IQ points. That is the same hit as losing a full night of sleep."
- Land: "Overwhelm is not just a feeling. It is a tax on the mind, charged in real time."

**Your receipts:** Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir and Zhao (Science, 2013) found that triggering financial worry reduced cognitive performance by the equivalent of about 13 IQ points, or a night of lost sleep ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1238041)). Caveat: it is "bandwidth," situational, and it lifts when the pressure lifts. Not permanent IQ loss.

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "Being broke literally nerfs your brain. It is a documented debuff."
- Millennials: "You are not bad with money, your bandwidth is taxed by the stress of not having any."
- Gen X: "Worry eats the exact mental capacity you needed to fix the worry."
- Boomers: "A mind full of money trouble has no room left to think straight."

**The fix (CTA):** "When you are under strain, cut the number of decisions and offload everything you can to lists and autopay." You are not failing, your bandwidth is taxed, so shrink the load on it and protect your thinking for what matters.

**Keep them watching:** Hook with "stress lowers your IQ in real time," then reveal the 13 point number.

## THE 47-SECOND ATTENTION SPAN

**The tension:** Your attention on a screen got nearly four times shorter in twenty years, and half of it is your own doing.

**Audio lines (read in order):**
- Hook: "The average person now holds attention on a screen for about 47 seconds. Twenty years ago it was two and a half minutes."
- Build: "That collapse was tracked in real research, not a goldfish meme. We trained ourselves to flit."
- Turn: "And here is the uncomfortable half. Roughly half of those interruptions are not pings. They are you, switching on your own."
- Land: "We did not just lose focus to the phone. We internalized the urge to break it ourselves."

**Your receipts:** Gloria Mark's research found average on-screen attention fell from about 2.5 minutes in 2004 to about 47 seconds, and that roughly half of interruptions are self-initiated ([Gloria Mark, Attention Span](https://gloriamark.com/attention-span/)). Caveat: ignore the "8 second goldfish" claim, which has no real source; the often-cited "23 minutes to refocus" should be attributed loosely to Mark, not as a precise lab constant.

**Every generation:**
- Gen Z: "47 second attention span and half of it is self-sabotage. Relatable."
- Millennials: "We check the phone for no reason now. The buzz is internal."
- Gen X: "Cannot sit with one task for two minutes, and the phone did not even ring."
- Boomers: "People cannot focus for a minute now, and they interrupt themselves."

**The fix (CTA):** "When your hand reaches for the phone with no notification, just notice it and put it back. That is the self-interruption you can actually control." Stack one 20 minute single-task block a day and grow it. You can retrain the focus you trained away.

**Keep them watching:** Hook on the 47 seconds, then twist to "half of it is you."
